### 1.1 The Collapse of the Presolar Nebula
Approximately 4.6 billion years ago, a fragment of a giant interstellar molecular cloud (mostly hydrogen, helium, and interstellar dust enriched by prior supernovae) collapsed under its own gravity.
- **Conservation of Angular Momentum**: As the cloud contracted from light-years across to astronomical unit scales, its rotational velocity increased ($L = I \omega = \text{constant}$). Centrifugal force balanced gravity perpendicular to the rotation axis, flattening the cloud into a spinning **Protoplanetary Disk** with the dense Proto-Sun at the center.

### 1.2 The Frost Line (The Ice Line): Dividing Rocky and Gas Planets
The thermal gradient across the protoplanetary disk dictated which materials could condense into solids:
- Inside **$\sim 2.7\text{ AU}$** (the **Frost Line**, located between Mars and Jupiter), temperatures were $>150\text{ K}$. Only high-melting-point **silicates, iron, and nickel** could condense. Because metals and rock represent $<0.6\%$ of the nebula's mass, inner planets remained small and rocky: **Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars**.
- Outside **$2.7\text{ AU}$**, temperatures dropped below the freezing point of water ($150\text{ K}$ in vacuum), methane, and ammonia. Volatile ices were $10\times$ more abundant than rock. Large icy cores grew to 10–20 Earth masses and gravitationally accreted massive envelopes of hydrogen and helium gas before the solar wind blew the disk away: **Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune**.

### 2.1 The Origin of Moons: Three Distinct Mechanisms
1. **Co-Accretion from Circumplanetary Disks**: Formed in miniature accretion disks around gas giants, orbiting in the equatorial plane with low eccentricity (e.g., Jupiter’s Galilean moons: Io, Europa, Ganymede, Callisto).
2. **Gravitational Capture**: Stray asteroids or Kuiper Belt objects captured into irregular, high-inclination or retrograde orbits (e.g., Neptune’s giant moon **Triton**, Mars’s moons **Phobos and Deimos**).
3. **The Giant Impact Hypothesis (Earth's Moon)**: Approximately 4.51 billion years ago, a Mars-sized protoplanet named **Theia** collided with the proto-Earth at an oblique angle. The impact vaporized portions of Earth’s mantle and Theia, ejecting a ring of debris into orbit that accreted into the Moon within a few thousand years.

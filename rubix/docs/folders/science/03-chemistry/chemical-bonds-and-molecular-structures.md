## 1. The Three Primary Chemical Bonds

```
+───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────+
|                                      THE PRIMARY CHEMICAL BOND TAXONOMY                                       |
+──────────────────────────+────────────────────────────────────+───────────────────────────────────────────────+
| Bond Type                | Physical Mechanism                 | Characteristics & Examples                    |
+──────────────────────────+────────────────────────────────────+───────────────────────────────────────────────+
| 1. Covalent Bond         | Shared electron pairs in orbitals  | Directional; Δχ < 1.7; H₂, CH₄, Diamond       |
| 2. Ionic Bond            | Electrostatic attraction: M⁺ & X⁻  | Non-directional; high m.p.; NaCl, MgO, CaF₂   |
| 3. Metallic Bond         | Delocalized valence electron sea   | Electrical/thermal conductor; Fe, Cu, Au, Al  |
+──────────────────────────+────────────────────────────────────+───────────────────────────────────────────────+
```

### 1.1 Covalent Bonding: Orbitals, $\sigma$ and $\pi$ Overlaps
When two non-metal atoms share valence electrons to achieve stable noble gas octets:
- **Sigma ($\sigma$) Bond**: Head-on, cylindrical symmetrical overlap of atomic orbitals along the internuclear axis. Free rotation is permitted around single $\sigma$ bonds.
- **Pi ($\pi$) Bond**: Lateral, side-by-side overlap of parallel $p$ orbitals above and below the internuclear axis. Present in double bonds (1 $\sigma$ + 1 $\pi$) and triple bonds (1 $\sigma$ + 2 $\pi$). **Rigid planar geometry**: rotation around a $\pi$ bond is restricted, giving rise to *cis-trans* isomerism.
- **Orbital Hybridization (Linus Pauling, 1931 Nobel Prize)**:
  - **$sp^3$ Hybridization**: One $2s$ and three $2p$ orbitals mix to form four equivalent tetrahedral hybrid orbitals at **109.5°** angles (e.g., Methane $\text{CH}_4$, Diamond).
  - **$sp^2$ Hybridization**: One $2s$ and two $2p$ orbitals mix to form three trigonal planar orbitals at **120°**, leaving one unhybridized $p$ orbital for a $\pi$ bond (e.g., Ethylene $\text{C}_2\text{H}_4$, Graphene).
  - **$sp$ Hybridization**: One $2s$ and one $2p$ orbital mix to form two linear orbitals at **180°**, leaving two unhybridized $p$ orbitals for two $\pi$ bonds (e.g., Acetylene $\text{C}_2\text{H}_2$).

### 1.2 Ionic Bonding: Electrostatic Lattices
When electronegativity difference $\Delta \chi > 1.7$ (e.g., $\text{Na}$ with $\chi = 0.9$ and $\text{Cl}$ with $\chi = 3.0$), an electron is completely transferred from the metal to the non-metal:
$$\text{Na} + \text{Cl} \longrightarrow \text{Na}^+ + \text{Cl}^-$$
- Ions pack into a repeating 3D crystalline lattice governed by **Coulomb’s Law**:
  $$E_{\text{lattice}} \propto \frac{q_1 q_2}{r_0}$$
- Hard, brittle, high melting points ($801^\circ\text{C}$ for NaCl), insulators as solids, but conduct electricity when molten or dissolved in water.

## 2. Molecular Geometry: VSEPR Theory

The **Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion (VSEPR)** theory (Gillespie and Nyholm, 1957) dictates that electron pairs (bonding pairs and lone pairs) surrounding a central atom repel each other electrostaticly, adopting geometries that maximize spatial separation:

```
+───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────+
|                                      VSEPR MOLECULAR GEOMETRIES                                               |
+────────────────────+─────────────+────────────+──────────────+─────────────────+──────────────────────────────+
| Steric Number (SN) | Bond Pairs  | Lone Pairs | Ideal Angle  | Molecular Shape | Exemplary Molecule           |
+────────────────────+─────────────+────────────+──────────────+─────────────────+──────────────────────────────+
| 2                  | 2           | 0          | 180°         | Linear          | Carbon Dioxide (CO₂), BeCl₂  |
| 3                  | 3           | 0          | 120°         | Trigonal Planar | Boron Trifluoride (BF₃)      |
| 3                  | 2           | 1          | ~118°        | Bent            | Sulfur Dioxide (SO₂)         |
| 4                  | 4           | 0          | 109.5°       | Tetrahedral     | Methane (CH₄), CCl₄          |
| 4                  | 3           | 1          | 107.0°       | Trigonal Pyram. | Ammonia (NH₃)                |
| 4                  | 2           | 2          | 104.5°       | Bent            | Water (H₂O)                  |
| 5                  | 5           | 0          | 90°, 120°    | Trigonal Bipyr. | Phosphorus Pentachloride(PCl₅|
| 6                  | 6           | 0          | 90°          | Octahedral      | Sulfur Hexafluoride (SF₆)    |
+────────────────────+─────────────+────────────+──────────────+─────────────────+──────────────────────────────+
```

- **Why Water is Bent (104.5°)**: Oxygen has 2 bonding pairs with hydrogen and 2 unshared lone pairs. Lone pairs occupy more angular volume than bonding pairs because they are held by only one positive nucleus, squeezing the ideal $109.5^\circ$ tetrahedral angle down to $104.5^\circ$.

## 3. Intermolecular Forces: The Glue of Liquids, Solids & Life

Intermolecular forces (IMFs) are attractive forces *between* separate molecules, weaker than covalent bonds (typically 1–40 kJ/mol vs 400 kJ/mol):

```
+───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────+
|                                    INTERMOLECULAR FORCES HIERARCHY                                            |
+──────────────────────────+────────────────────+───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────+
| Force Type               | Relative Strength  | Physical Origin & Universal Significance                      |
+──────────────────────────+────────────────────+───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────+
| 1. Hydrogen Bonding      | Strongest IMF (40kJ| Permanent dipole: H covalently bonded to high-χ F, O, or N    |
| 2. Dipole-Dipole         | Moderate (5-25 kJ) | Attraction between permanent opposite molecular dipoles       |
| 3. London Dispersion     | Weak (0.1-5 kJ/mol)| Temporary induced instantaneous quantum electronic dipoles   |
+──────────────────────────+────────────────────+───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────+
```

### 3.1 Hydrogen Bonding: The Prerequisite for Life on Earth
- A hydrogen atom bonded to a highly electronegative atom (Oxygen, Nitrogen, or Fluorine) is stripped of its electron cloud, exposing its naked positive proton. This exposed proton forms an electrostatic bridge to the lone pair of an adjacent molecule.
- **The Anomalies of Water ($H_2O$)**:
  1. Water has an extraordinarily high boiling point ($100^\circ\text{C}$ vs $-60^\circ\text{C}$ for $H_2S$, which has identical geometry and higher molecular weight).
  2. **Density Inversion of Ice**: Solid ice is **less dense than liquid water** because hydrogen bonds lock water molecules into an open hexagonal crystal lattice at $<4^\circ\text{C}$. This causes ice to float on lakes and oceans, insulating marine life below and preventing entire oceans from freezing solid.
  3. **DNA Double Helix Stability**: Hydrogen bonds hold the base pairs of DNA together ($A=T$ shares 2 hydrogen bonds, $G\equiv C$ shares 3 hydrogen bonds). They are strong enough to maintain genetic stability, yet weak enough to be unzipped by cellular helicase enzymes for replication and transcription.
